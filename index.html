About Me

I am an early-career researcher in International Relations specializing in international security, transnational illicit economies, and non-state governance. I hold a Master of Arts in International Relations (Security) from LUISS Guido Carli (graduating with 109/110) and a Bachelor’s degree from Fundação Getulio Vargas (FGV).

My research investigates how non-state and hybrid actors monetize circulation across peripheral borderlands to construct durable structures of rule. Combining qualitative Bayesian process tracing with formal economic modeling, my work examines how economic extraction translates into coercive and institutional capacity.

Flagship Project | Master's Thesis

Ordered Disorder: Sovereign Rent Conversion and the Political Economy of Circulation in the Central Sahara

LUISS Guido Carli (2026) | Supervisors: Prof. Andrea Capati & Prof. Michele Petrone

This dissertation investigates how durable political order emerges in peripheral borderlands characterized by fragmented sovereignty. Challenging conventional state-centric theories, the study introduces the Sovereign Rent Conversion Model (SRCM), demonstrating that hybrid political authority is generated endogenously through the regulation, taxation, and protection of trans-Saharan circulation across the Niger–Libya corridor.

Prospective Doctoral Research Agenda

Building upon the Sovereign Rent Conversion Model (SRCM), my prospective doctoral research aims to advance comparative theories of state formation and security governance through three primary extensions:

1. Comparative Borderland Authority (Latin America vs. the Sahel)

Extending the SRCM framework to examine how non-state armed groups and criminal cartels govern transit corridors and cross-border migration routes in regions such as the Darién Gap or the Mexico–U.S. borderlands.

2. The Micro-Foundations of Relational Contingency in War Economies

Investigating how local kinship networks, customary authorities, and religious institutions mediate conversion efficiency and shape the long-term institutional survival of armed governance across fragile state margins.

3. The Macro-Politics of Border Externalization

A comparative analysis evaluating how European and North American border externalization policies unintentionally incentivize the professionalization of protection markets and institutionalize hybrid criminal-state governance across transit peripheries.
