01 / Profile

Researching authority beyond the state

I am an early-career International Relations researcher interested in how political authority is built and sustained where formal sovereignty is fragmented.

I hold an M.A. in International Relations (Security) from LUISS Guido Carli, graduating with 109/110, and a bachelor's degree from Fundação Getulio Vargas (FGV). My research examines how actors operating across borderlands regulate the movement of people, goods, and capital; and how the rents associated with that circulation can support durable forms of governance.

My master's thesis develops a conceptual framework for understanding this process in the Central Sahara, focusing on the Niger–Libya corridor. My broader interests include transnational illicit economies, war economies, security governance, and the relationship between state and non-state authority.

02 / Selected work

Research portfolio

Master's thesis LUISS Guido Carli · 2026

Ordered Disorder: Sovereign Rent Conversion and the Political Economy of Circulation in the Central Sahara

Supervisors: Prof. Andrea Capati and Prof. Michele Petrone

The thesis asks how durable political order can emerge where sovereignty is fragmented. Focusing on the Niger–Libya corridor, it introduces the Sovereign Rent Conversion Model (SRCM) to explain how authority may emerge through the regulation of transnational circulation rather than control of territory alone.

Abstract

Using qualitative Bayesian process tracing, the study examines how movement becomes concentrated at physical chokepoints, transforming mobility into scarcity rents that hybrid actors may convert into durable coercive and administrative capacity. Tracing the Niger–Libya axis—from Agadez's post-repeal transport economy to armed groups in Fezzan—the thesis argues that extra-legal hybrid regimes can be resilient features of peripheral governance rather than merely temporary symptoms of state failure.

Research approach: qualitative Bayesian process tracing, used to assess the proposed causal mechanism linking control over circulation, rent extraction, and political authority.

03 / Next questions

Prospective doctoral research agenda

These are exploratory directions for future research, not finalized dissertation proposals. They range from focused questions about cross-border governance and crime financing to broader interests in political violence, democracy, and security.

A

Border externalization and hybrid governance

Guiding question: How do external border-control policies reshape the incentives and authority of intermediaries operating along migration routes?

A comparative project could investigate whether and how policies implemented beyond a state's territory affect protection markets and relations between public authorities and non-state actors along transit routes.

B

Crime financing and the political economy of insecurity

Guiding question: How do illicit revenues and financing networks shape the organization, resilience, and political influence of criminal and armed actors?

This broader direction could examine how criminal markets, informal financial channels, and relationships between licit and illicit economies sustain organized crime or armed groups across different political settings. It would allow the research to move beyond a single region or type of armed governance while retaining a focus on the relationship between economic resources, coercive capacity, and political outcomes.

C

Political violence, democracy, and security governance

Guiding question: How do states and non-state actors shape patterns of political violence, and what are the consequences for democratic institutions and security governance?

This broader research interest could support future work on conflict, repression, democratic resilience, and the institutional responses to security threats. It is intentionally an umbrella direction rather than a narrowly defined dissertation proposal, and could be refined around a specific mechanism and comparative case selection.

D

Comparing borderland authority across regions

Guiding question: Under what conditions do actors who regulate transit routes turn control over circulation into durable local authority?

A potential comparative extension would examine the Central Sahara alongside a Latin American borderland, such as the Darién Gap. The comparison would need to specify which mechanisms are shared across the cases and which differences in state presence, geography, and political context limit the comparison.
